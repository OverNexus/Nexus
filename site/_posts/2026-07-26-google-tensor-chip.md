## What's Happening
Google's Tensor chipsets have been a topic of discussion since their introduction in 2021. The first-generation Tensor chip was used in the Pixel 6 series, which received mixed reviews. Despite its promising AI capabilities, the chip's performance was underwhelming, especially when compared to other flagship smartphones. The Tensor G2, used in the Pixel 7 series, showed some improvement but still fell short of expectations. Now, with the Tensor G6 in development, Google has a chance to redeem itself and prove that its custom chipsets are a force to be reckoned with.

The Tensor chip's struggles can be attributed to various factors, including thermal issues, lackluster GPU performance, and inadequate optimization. These problems have led to a perception that Google's custom chipsets are not on par with those from Qualcomm, Apple, or Samsung. However, it's essential to note that Google's approach to chip design is focused on AI and machine learning, which could potentially give it an edge in the long run. The company has been working to improve its chipmaking capabilities, and the Tensor G6 is expected to address some of the previous generation's shortcomings.

Google's decision to develop its own chipsets was a bold move, and it's not uncommon for new technologies to face teething issues. The company has been open about its commitment to improving the Tensor chip, and the upcoming Tensor G6 is expected to feature significant upgrades. With the help of its recent acquisitions, including the $1.1 billion purchase of part of HTC's smartphone team, Google is well-equipped to tackle the challenges ahead. The question remains, though: can Google finally get it right with the Tensor G6, or will it continue to struggle to keep up with the competition?

## Deep Dive
From a technical standpoint, the Tensor chip's architecture is designed to prioritize AI and machine learning tasks. This approach has allowed Google to optimize its chip for specific use cases, such as image processing and natural language processing. However, this focus has come at the cost of general performance, which has been a major point of contention. The Tensor G2, for example, featured a 5nm process node and a Mali-G710 MP7 GPU, which, while improved over its predecessor, still lagged behind other flagship chipsets.

In terms of benchmarks, the Tensor G2 scored around 830 in the Geekbench 5 single-core test and 2,800 in the multi-core test. While these numbers are respectable, they pale in comparison to the Apple A16 Bionic, which scored 1,330 in the single-core test and 4,600 in the multi-core test. The Qualcomm Snapdragon 8 Gen 2, used in many Android flagships, also outperformed the Tensor G2 in most benchmarks. Google needs to address these performance gaps if it wants the Tensor G6 to be competitive.

To achieve this, Google could consider adopting a more traditional approach to chip design, focusing on general performance while still maintaining its AI and machine learning advantages. This might involve partnering with other companies, such as AMD or NVIDIA, to develop a more robust GPU or licensing existing IP to improve the chip's overall performance. Alternatively, Google could continue to push the boundaries of AI-centric chip design, potentially creating a new paradigm for smartphone processors.
